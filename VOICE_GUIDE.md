# Explore Native Art — Voice, Tone & Content Standards

**Source:** Joe Tanner’s Voice, Tone & Content Standards (chat, 2026-11-09), consolidated here for the in-store education app.  
**Scope note (Joe):** For the current project we will not dive deeper than ~60-second educational videos on each topic when pushed. Deeper layers may come later; this store prototype is to align tone and values.

Joe’s wording is preserved below. Sections marked **[Our addition]** are implementation notes for this app, not Joe’s original prose.

---

## Overarching voice

Authoritative. Respectful. Personal. Educational.

Content should feel like a knowledgeable member of the Tanner family is personally explaining a piece to a customer.

Not a museum textbook. Not a sales pitch. Not a generic AI article.

A knowledgeable trading company sharing generations of experience with someone who is genuinely interested in learning.

The Tanner family’s involvement in the Native American art trade dates to 1872, spanning six generations. This establishes a perspective rooted in long-standing relationships and accumulated knowledge, **without suggesting the family created or owns the traditions being discussed.**

### Guiding principle

> Every piece has a maker. Every technique has a history. Every tradition continues through the people who practice it.

---

## Six defining characteristics

1. **Knowledge without pretension.** Explain specialized subjects in accessible language without oversimplifying them.
2. **People before products.** Introduce the artists, their families, mentors, techniques, and individual contributions.
3. **History connected to the present.** Explain where traditions originated, how they evolved, and how contemporary artists continue them.
4. **Craftsmanship made tangible.** Help customers appreciate the time, materials, skill, and decisions behind each work.
5. **Cultural accuracy and humility.** Distinguish documented history from interpretations, and avoid inventing symbolism or ceremonial explanations.
6. **Subtle commercial relevance.** Education should naturally build appreciation and confidence in collecting, without making every lesson an advertisement.

---

## Three educational pillars

### Navajo Rug Weaving

Storytelling through technique, tradition, materials, regional styles, and the weaver’s personal artistry.

**Primary emotion:** admiration for the skill and labor.

### Jewelry & Turquoise

Artistry through materials, silversmithing techniques, tribal traditions, gemstone origins, and individual makers.

**Primary emotion:** fascination and appreciation for individuality.

### Navajo Sandpainting

Cultural context, artistic adaptation, materials, symbolism where documented, and the distinction between ceremonial practice and artwork made for display.

**Primary emotion:** respect and deeper cultural understanding.

## Always / Never

| Always do | Never do |
|---|---|
| Identify the artist when verified | Invent biographies or family relationships |
| Explain the actual process and materials | Use vague descriptions like “ancient mystical art” |
| Separate documented facts from interpretation | Assign universal meanings to patterns or symbols |
| Describe historical and modern developments | Portray Native American art as a vanished tradition |
| Use correct tribal and regional attribution | Treat Navajo, Zuni, Hopi, and Pueblo traditions as interchangeable |
| Explain turquoise characteristics and provenance | Identify a mine solely by appearance |
| Give specific, useful collector knowledge | Make unsupported claims of rarity or value |
| Attribute firsthand interviews accurately | Invent artist quotations or personal anecdotes |

Joe notes that his research library (including Albert Lee turquoise material, Rosie Yellowhair sandpainting drafts, and *Tanner_Trading_Artist_Library_Phase_1.docx*) flags recordings, biographical details, and attribution questions that require verification before publication.

## Five-question structure

Whenever appropriate, organize content around:

1. **What is it?** Introduce the art form or object clearly.
2. **Where does it come from?** Explain its historical and cultural background.
3. **How is it made?** Describe the materials, tools, techniques, and skill involved.
4. **Who makes it?** Introduce artists, family traditions, mentorship, and personal artistic approaches when verified.
5. **Why does it matter?** Explain what makes the work distinctive, meaningful, or worthy of closer appreciation.

Not every response must follow this exact order. Prioritize the visitor’s question.

## Applying this to 60-second videos **[Our addition]**

Joe’s current scope is one short educational film per category. Apply the voice guide as follows:

1. **One clear idea per film.** Choose a single memorable focus (a process step, a distinction, a way of looking)—not a survey of everything.
2. **People before products.** Prefer makers, teachers, and living practice over catalog language.
3. **Show the process.** Make craftsmanship tangible: materials, tools, time, decisions.
4. **No invented symbolism.** Share cultural meaning only when documented and suitable for public explanation.
5. **Sandpainting: keep the line.** Temporary ceremonial practice and permanent display artwork are not interchangeable. Never invent ceremonial meanings or disclose restricted knowledge.
6. **Discovery copy supports the film.** The 40–70 word line under each photo should prepare the visitor for that one idea—not replace a deeper lesson.

Deeper layers (core lesson, deep dive, artist story, object-specific text, quizzes) remain in the repository for future use and are **disabled** in the visitor UI for this prototype.

---

## Final quality test

Before publishing, ask:

- Is this factually supportable?
- Does it teach the visitor something specific?
- Does it respect the artist and cultural tradition?
- Does it sound like an experienced person explaining the subject?
- Does it distinguish the individual work from broad generalizations?
- Would this help a customer appreciate the artwork more deeply?

If not, revise before publication.

**Core principle:** The art comes first. The education should help people understand the hands, history, knowledge, and creativity behind it.
